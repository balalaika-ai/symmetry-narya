## Corrections to the book

- example (no label), fingp.tex:27: The formalization proves these facts. Sigma_3 has a unique subgroup of order 3, <(0 1 2)>, and this subgroup is normal. Sigma_4 has 1 or 4 subgroups of order 3 (3-Sylow), and they are all conjugate. In Sigma_4, <(0 1 2)> is not normal, so there are exactly 4. Also, 9 \| n! for n > 5. For n > 5, the subgroups of order 3 of Sigma_n are not all conjugate. Module 1052 shows this with <(0 1 2)> and <(0 1 2)(3 4 5)> in Sigma_(m+6). The proof counts the moved points: 3 for the first subgroup and 6 for the second. <(0 1 2)> and <(3 4 5)> are conjugate. The formalization refutes three printed claims with formal counterexamples (Bridges, chapters/ch10/blind). The claim 'Sigma_n has n(n-1)(n-2)/6 subgroups of order 3' fails at n = 6. The reason: the number of subgroups of order p of a group with p \| \|G\| is = 1 mod p (order_p_subgroups_congruent, module 1051), and 20 is not. The claim '<(1 2 3)> is not conjugate to <(4 5 6)>' is false. The parenthetical 'one for each subset of cardinality 3' is false: binomial(6,3) = 20 is not = 1 mod 3, but \|Syl_3(Sigma_6)\| is. The number 10 of subgroups of order 9 is right (720/72); a complementary pair of 3-element subsets gives the same subgroup. Modules 1053-1054 prove the exact numbers of subgroups of order 3 by enumeration. Sigma_3, Sigma_4, Sigma_5 and Sigma_6 have 1, 4, 10 and 40 (sigma5_order_three_subgroups_card, sigma6_order_three_subgroups_card). The book's formula counts only the subgroups generated by a 3-cycle.
